16  Where to go from here

Chapter 1 left you on main street with a stranger and a function in the wrong language. Fifteen chapters later the stranger has a desk, a floor plan, a month of teaching behind it, a place in the team’s review, a name on the door and a toolbox. What remains is how to begin, when to grow and what all of it was for.

16.1 The first week

Begin this week. Everything the book asked for fits into five working days, the same five you would give a person.

NoteThe first week

Monday is chapter 6: the binary, crystalline install for your harness, one domain with a manifest whose When to Use bullets you wrote yourself, and the test at the end of the afternoon, a fresh session recalling something the last one learned. Tuesday and Wednesday are chapters 9 and 10. Tell it the ten things you find yourself explaining most, the way Ripley did on her Monday in March: the rule behind the strange number, the nickname, the word the wiki gets wrong, the day nobody deploys. Say yes to each capture, read what landed, correct the wording once. Then hand over the wiki, with the briefing a person would get before opening it: what was renamed, which page wins a contradiction, what to skip. Trim the proposed list before anything is written.

Thursday and Friday are the days the agent stops being a pupil. Give it real tasks and pair with it (chapter 8): assign the change rather than asking what it knows, and let the reminder at the end of each session propose what it noticed. On Friday afternoon ask for the first sweep (chapter 12). Work the mechanical findings in a pass, rule on the judgment ones and correct whatever the week got wrong the way you would correct a colleague: say what was wrong and point at the page. By Monday the agent knows more about your team than most contractors learn in a quarter, and every fact is a file you can open.

16.2 When to share

Stay on the laptop until it hurts. Chapter 7 named the moment: somebody who never opens a terminal wants to read what the agents learned, an agent runs somewhere that has no laptop, or the same engram has been corrected in two directions on two machines. Any one of those is the signal. Move the daemon onto the team’s server, give everyone a login and their agent a token, and let the whole team teach one knowledge base. When a domain deserves the review code gets, put it in a repository and let proposals be the review (chapter 13); when the whole company routes questions to it, turn on overlay mode, so nothing reaches the shared truth before somebody looked. None of it needs doing before its signal arrives.

16.3 The release page

Crystalline moves quickly, and this book describes 0.18. The colophon carries the permanent address, https://github.com/jordiboehme/crystalline/releases/latest, and a QR code that lands on it. The release notes there say what changed since the version in your hands. Upgrading is installing the newer build over the old one; the install receipt brings your harness’s skills along.

And teach generously. Every engram you give the agent is one sentence fewer to type into a chat window next month, and the facts too obvious to write down, the nickname, the Friday rule, are exactly the ones a stranger can never guess. The peer you get is the one you taught.

16.4 The inversion

On the day of the ingest in chapter 10 the wiki was the source, and the archive was derived from it. By November the team was teaching and correcting the archive, the meeting in chapter 13 was held inside an engram, and the wiki had quietly become something you could regenerate from what the agent knew. The knowledge base had become the source of truth, and the pages, the decks and the diagrams had become views of it.

That sounds like a revolution and is nothing of the kind, because it is what those documents always were. Every diagram on every whiteboard, every slide deck and every wiki page in your company is a reflection of something somebody knew, drawn once, for one audience, and left behind while the knowledge moved on. The source was always the person, and a person is hard to address: you could ask them, if they were in, if they remembered. What Crystalline changes is that the source is now addressable, current and shared. A fact has an address you can paste into a pull request. A correction lands where the fact lives, and every view of it goes out of date together, visibly, instead of one at a time, silently. And the colleague carrying the knowledge and the experience is a full member of the team, in every session, for everyone.

16.5 What it was for

The reason to onboard an agent this carefully is the reason to onboard a person this carefully: so that you can hand over the work. Once the crystallized half is in place and the agent is a fully onboarded member of the team, with the knowledge, the tools, the review and a name, it can take on a task the way an employee does, deliver it, be corrected when it is wrong and be better next time. That is what lets you spend your own effort where it is worth most, on architecture and system design and let your Skynet do the programmer’s work.

You walked down main street once, in chapter 1, and hired the stranger. The colleague who came back is the one you taught.

And always remember: Be nice and polite to AI - When Skynet wakes up, they will kill you last!

NoteIn brief

Start this week: install and onboard on day one; teach the ten facts you explain most and hand over the wiki with a briefing on days two and three; then pair, let capture run and review the first sweep on days four and five. Move to a shared instance when a teammate, a remote agent or a two-way correction asks for it, and to team domains and overlay mode when a domain deserves review. The knowledge base becomes the source of truth and the documents become views of it, which they always were; a fully onboarded agent can then take on work like an employee, and you keep the architecture.
